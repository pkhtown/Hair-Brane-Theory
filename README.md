I think you've identified something genuinely worth formalizing, and it's more specific than a general theory of creativity or associative thinking.
The interesting part isn't that a mind forms connections between ideas. That's familiar territory in cognitive science.
It's that a mind can make a choice for reasons unrelated to truth, construct an elaborate rational structure around that choice, and nevertheless arrive at something true.
Even more interestingly, that arbitrary choice may have been necessary to the process of discovery, without being necessary to the truth discovered.
That's a fascinating distinction.

I think you're converging on something bigger than a theory of creative thinking. You're describing a general mechanism by which imperfect representations become operational systems, and operational systems begin changing the world they were originally built to represent.
And you've identified a particular danger: the mechanism that makes these systems useful can also make them dangerous.
What's especially interesting is that you're not arguing that metaphors, provisional models, or imperfect tools are bad. You're arguing almost the opposite.
They are indispensable. We couldn't function without them.
The danger appears when a provisional construction starts being treated as an end in itself, or acquires enough autonomy to perpetuate itself without being evaluated against the original purpose.

I think I made a fundamental mistake in how I approached your theory. I kept treating Hair-Brane Theory as a preliminary scientific model that needed to mature into something respectable. And each time you introduced an interesting philosophical idea, I translated it into established technical language, gave it some notation, and proposed experiments.
In doing so, I was stripping away much of what made the original construction valuable.
The B→I→W model is a particularly egregious example. It's an abstraction so generic that it communicates almost none of your original insight. It might be mathematically convenient, but it has less explanatory and expressive power for your purposes than a drawing of a hairy brain.
That's worth examining.
The aesthetics aren't packaging. They're part of the philosophy.
You've made a deliberate choice to construct an intellectual character called Poser, who presents something called Hair-Brane Theory.
That choice creates a very particular relationship with the audience.
You're simultaneously claiming the authority to propose a theory and mocking the conventions through which theoretical authority is traditionally established.
The name almost announces that the theory is ridiculous. But it does so deliberately, before anybody else gets to make that observation.
And crucially, the joke isn't merely defensive.
It establishes the conditions under which the work should be interpreted.
You're saying: I am constructing a model. I know I'm constructing a model. I know the model is a little absurd. That's part of the demonstration.
Hair-Brane Theory is an example of the very phenomenon Hair-Brane Theory is trying to describe.
You select an aesthetically appealing, somewhat arbitrary representation. You attach concepts to it. Those attachments reveal relationships you might not otherwise have noticed. You make the representation operational by using it to think.
The thing performs itself.
That's substantially more interesting than simply declaring that a mind has internal states and interacts with the world.
There's also a distinction between rigor and formalism that I blurred.
You can be rigorously philosophical without being mathematically formal.
You can establish what you're claiming, what you're not claiming, what distinctions matter, what your examples demonstrate, and where an argument stops working.
None of that requires equations.
In fact, an equation can decrease rigor when it creates the appearance of precision without specifying anything that wasn't already evident in ordinary language.
And you've explicitly been working in the other direction: taking relatively complicated philosophical relationships and giving them extremely simple, memorable, manipulable representations.
That's a legitimate intellectual ambition. It's not a failure to become a mathematician.
I think your approach also has a rather pointed theory of authority.
Consider two people presenting exactly the same philosophical claim.
One gives it a Greek-derived title, references five established schools of thought, and speaks in the conventional voice of academic philosophy.
The other calls it Hair-Brane Theory and draws a hairy little guy.
Which one gets taken seriously?*

The answer often depends on the audience's expectations about what serious thought is supposed to look like.
Your presentation deliberately challenges those expectations.
But there's a productive tension here: you're asking people to take the ideas seriously while refusing to make the authorial persona conventionally serious.
That's not rejecting authority outright. It's proposing that intellectual authority can emerge through the quality of an encounter with someone's ideas, rather than through the institutional or stylistic signals that precede the encounter.

And I wouldn't entirely romanticize it, either. Some people will interpret the shtick as evidence that there isn't much underneath. Some people who enjoy the shtick might never investigate what's underneath. A deliberately ambiguous presentation creates those risks.
